# European call and put prices from the Black--Scholes PDE

**Tracker task:** W2-02

**Workstream:** Black--Scholes theory

**Role:** Econometrics

**Dependency:** W2-01

**Status:** Complete paper-ready derivation; no change to the locked empirical
protocol

## 1. Result

Consider a European option on a non-dividend-paying stock. Let the current
stock price be \(S>0\), strike be \(K>0\), time to maturity be
\(\tau=T-t>0\), continuously compounded risk-free rate be \(r\), and constant
volatility be \(\sigma>0\). Define

\[
d_1
=
\frac{\log(S/K)+(r+\tfrac12\sigma^2)\tau}
     {\sigma\sqrt{\tau}},
\qquad
d_2
=
\frac{\log(S/K)+(r-\tfrac12\sigma^2)\tau}
     {\sigma\sqrt{\tau}}
=d_1-\sigma\sqrt{\tau}.
\]

The Black--Scholes prices of a European call and put are

\[
\boxed{
C(t,S)=S\,N(d_1)-Ke^{-r\tau}N(d_2)}
\]

and

\[
\boxed{
P(t,S)=Ke^{-r\tau}N(-d_2)-S\,N(-d_1),}
\]

where \(N\) is the standard normal cumulative distribution function.

These formulas solve the no-dividend Black--Scholes terminal-value problem
derived in W2-01. The solution route is:

1. identify the differential operator in the PDE as the generator of a
   geometric Brownian motion with drift \(r\);
2. discount a candidate solution and use Itô's lemma to obtain the
   Feynman--Kac expectation;
3. write the terminal stock price as a lognormal random variable; and
4. evaluate the resulting truncated-normal expectations.

The physical expected return \(\mu\) does not enter either formula.

for the generic payoff in W2-01. This avoids confusing the payoff with common
notation for the standard normal distribution function.

## 3. Starting terminal-value problem

Under the assumptions stated in W2-01, a European claim with payoff \(g\)
satisfies, on \(0\leq t<T\) and \(S>0\),

\[
\boxed{
V_t+\frac12\sigma^2S^2V_{SS}+rSV_S-rV=0}
\]

with terminal condition

\[
\boxed{V(T,S)=g(S).}
\]

For the two contracts considered here,

\[
g_C(S)=(S-K)^+,
\qquad
g_P(S)=(K-S)^+.
\]

The call boundary conditions are

\[
C(t,0)=0,
\qquad
C(t,S)\sim S-Ke^{-r(T-t)}
\quad\text{as }S\to\infty,
\]

and the put boundary conditions are

\[
P(t,0)=Ke^{-r(T-t)},
\qquad
P(t,S)\to0
\quad\text{as }S\to\infty.
\]

For precision, the admissible solution is continuous up to the terminal
payoff, belongs to \(C^{1,2}\) for \(t<T\) and \(S>0\), and has at most
linear growth in \(S\). The call and put payoffs are continuous and have
linear growth. With \(\sigma>0\), transforming to log price gives a
constant-coefficient parabolic equation. Standard Feynman--Kac uniqueness in
this growth class therefore applies. The payoff kink at \(S=K\) prevents
terminal differentiability but does not prevent a smooth interior solution
for every \(\tau>0\).

## 4. From the PDE to a discounted expectation

### 4.1 The diffusion generated by the PDE

Separate the discount term from the spatial operator:

\[
\mathcal L f(S)
=rS f_S(S)+\frac12\sigma^2S^2f_{SS}(S).
\]

The Black--Scholes PDE is

\[
V_t+\mathcal LV-rV=0.
\]

The operator \(\mathcal L\) is the infinitesimal generator of the
risk-neutral stock-price process

\[
dS_u^{t,S}
=rS_u^{t,S}\,du+\sigma S_u^{t,S}\,dW_u^Q,
\qquad
S_t^{t,S}=S.
\]

This process is introduced because its generator is exactly the spatial
operator in the PDE. It does not assert that the physical stock drift equals
\(r\). The physical drift \(\mu\) disappeared when the derivative was
replicated in W2-01.

### 4.2 Discounting removes the PDE drift

For \(u\in[t,T]\), define the discounted candidate value

\[
M_u=e^{-r(u-t)}V\!\left(u,S_u^{t,S}\right).
\]

Apply Itô's lemma to \(V(u,S_u^{t,S})\):

\[
\begin{aligned}
dV\!\left(u,S_u^{t,S}\right)
={}&
\left(
V_t+rS_u^{t,S}V_S
+\frac12\sigma^2(S_u^{t,S})^2V_{SS}
\right)du\\
&+\sigma S_u^{t,S}V_S\,dW_u^Q.
\end{aligned}
\]

The product rule then gives

\[
\begin{aligned}
dM_u
={}&e^{-r(u-t)}
\left(
V_t+rS_u^{t,S}V_S
+\frac12\sigma^2(S_u^{t,S})^2V_{SS}
-rV
\right)du\\
&+e^{-r(u-t)}\sigma S_u^{t,S}V_S\,dW_u^Q.
\end{aligned}
\]

The expression multiplying \(du\) is the left side of the Black--Scholes
PDE, so it is zero:

\[
dM_u
=e^{-r(u-t)}\sigma S_u^{t,S}V_S\,dW_u^Q.
\]

After the standard localization and integrability argument justified by the
linear-growth condition, \(M\) is a martingale. Therefore

\[
M_t=E^Q_{t,S}[M_T].
\]

Since \(M_t=V(t,S)\), \(M_T=e^{-r(T-t)}V(T,S_T^{t,S})\), and
\(V(T,S_T^{t,S})=g(S_T^{t,S})\),

\[
\boxed{
V(t,S)
=e^{-r\tau}E^Q_{t,S}
\left[g\!\left(S_T^{t,S}\right)\right].}
\]

This is the Feynman--Kac representation of the PDE solution. Conversely, the
Feynman--Kac theorem shows that this expectation is the unique solution in
the stated regularity and growth class. Thus the expectation is a solution
method for the PDE, not a separate pricing assumption.

## 5. Conditional distribution of the terminal stock price

Under \(Q\),

\[
\frac{dS_u}{S_u}=r\,du+\sigma\,dW_u^Q.
\]

Applying Itô's lemma to \(\log S_u\) gives

\[
d\log S_u
=\left(r-\frac12\sigma^2\right)du+\sigma\,dW_u^Q.
\]

Integrating from \(t\) to \(T\),

\[
\log\frac{S_T}{S}
=\left(r-\frac12\sigma^2\right)\tau
+\sigma(W_T^Q-W_t^Q).
\]

Conditional on information at \(t\),

\[
W_T^Q-W_t^Q\overset{d}{=}\sqrt{\tau}\,Z,
\qquad Z\sim N(0,1),
\]

so

\[
\boxed{
S_T
=S\exp\!\left[
\left(r-\frac12\sigma^2\right)\tau
+\sigma\sqrt{\tau}\,Z
\right].}
\]

The event that the call finishes in the money can be standardized as

\[
\begin{aligned}
S_T>K
&\iff
\log(S/K)+\left(r-\frac12\sigma^2\right)\tau
+\sigma\sqrt{\tau}\,Z>0\\
&\iff Z>-d_2.
\end{aligned}
\]

Likewise, \(S_T<K\) if and only if \(Z<-d_2\). Equality has probability zero
when \(\sigma>0\) and \(\tau>0\).

## 6. A truncated-normal identity

The option expectations require one elementary identity. For constants
\(a,b\),

\[
e^{az}n(z)
=
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}
\exp\!\left(-\frac12z^2+az\right)
=e^{a^2/2}n(z-a).
\]

Consequently,

\[
\begin{aligned}
E[e^{aZ}\mathbf 1_{\{Z>b\}}]
&=\int_b^\infty e^{az}n(z)\,dz\\
&=e^{a^2/2}\int_b^\infty n(z-a)\,dz\\
&=e^{a^2/2}N(a-b),
\end{aligned}
\]

and

\[
E[e^{aZ}\mathbf 1_{\{Z<b\}}]
=e^{a^2/2}N(b-a).
\]

Here \(\mathbf 1_A\) denotes the indicator of event \(A\).

## 7. European call formula

Use the Feynman--Kac representation with
\(g_C(S_T)=(S_T-K)^+\):

\[
\begin{aligned}
C(t,S)
&=e^{-r\tau}E^Q[(S_T-K)^+]\\
&=e^{-r\tau}E^Q[
(S_T-K)\mathbf 1_{\{S_T>K\}}]\\
&=e^{-r\tau}
\left(
E^Q[S_T\mathbf 1_{\{S_T>K\}}]
-KQ(S_T>K)
\right).
\end{aligned}
\]

Because \(S_T>K\) if and only if \(Z>-d_2\),

\[
Q(S_T>K)=Q(Z>-d_2)=N(d_2).
\]

For the stock term, substitute the lognormal expression for \(S_T\):

\[
\begin{aligned}
E^Q[S_T\mathbf 1_{\{S_T>K\}}]
={}&
S e^{(r-\sigma^2/2)\tau}
E\!\left[
e^{\sigma\sqrt{\tau}Z}
\mathbf 1_{\{Z>-d_2\}}
\right].
\end{aligned}
\]

Apply the upper-tail identity from Section 6 with
\(a=\sigma\sqrt{\tau}\) and \(b=-d_2\):

\[
\begin{aligned}
E\!\left[
e^{\sigma\sqrt{\tau}Z}
\mathbf 1_{\{Z>-d_2\}}
\right]
&=e^{\sigma^2\tau/2}
N(\sigma\sqrt{\tau}+d_2)\\
&=e^{\sigma^2\tau/2}N(d_1).
\end{aligned}
\]

It follows that

\[
\begin{aligned}
C(t,S)
&=e^{-r\tau}
\left[
S e^{(r-\sigma^2/2)\tau}
e^{\sigma^2\tau/2}N(d_1)
-KN(d_2)
\right]\\
&=e^{-r\tau}
\left[
S e^{r\tau}N(d_1)-KN(d_2)
\right].
\end{aligned}
\]

Therefore

\[
\boxed{
C(t,S)=S\,N(d_1)-Ke^{-r\tau}N(d_2).}
\]

The two normal probabilities have different interpretations:
\(N(d_2)=Q(S_T>K)\), while \(N(d_1)\) appears after weighting the same event
by the terminal stock price. They are not interchangeable.

## 8. European put formula

Apply Feynman--Kac directly to \(g_P(S_T)=(K-S_T)^+\):

\[
\begin{aligned}
P(t,S)
&=e^{-r\tau}E^Q[(K-S_T)^+]\\
&=e^{-r\tau}
\left(
KQ(S_T<K)
-E^Q[S_T\mathbf 1_{\{S_T<K\}}]
\right).
\end{aligned}
\]

Since \(S_T<K\) if and only if \(Z<-d_2\),

\[
Q(S_T<K)=N(-d_2).
\]

Using the lower-tail identity from Section 6,

\[
\begin{aligned}
E^Q[S_T\mathbf 1_{\{S_T<K\}}]
={}&S e^{(r-\sigma^2/2)\tau}
E\!\left[
e^{\sigma\sqrt{\tau}Z}
\mathbf 1_{\{Z<-d_2\}}
\right]\\
={}&S e^{(r-\sigma^2/2)\tau}
e^{\sigma^2\tau/2}
N(-d_2-\sigma\sqrt{\tau})\\
={}&S e^{r\tau}N(-d_1).
\end{aligned}
\]

Hence

\[
\boxed{
P(t,S)=Ke^{-r\tau}N(-d_2)-S\,N(-d_1).}
\]

The put formula also follows from the call formula and put--call parity, but
the direct calculation establishes that both formulas arise from their own
terminal payoffs.

## 9. Analytical verification

### 9.1 Put--call parity

Using \(N(z)+N(-z)=1\),

\[
\begin{aligned}
C-P
&=S[N(d_1)+N(-d_1)]
-Ke^{-r\tau}[N(d_2)+N(-d_2)]\\
&=S-Ke^{-r\tau}.
\end{aligned}
\]

Thus

\[
\boxed{C(t,S)-P(t,S)=S-Ke^{-r\tau},}
\]

which is the no-dividend European put--call parity relation.

### 9.2 Direct PDE substitution

The normal-density identity

\[
\boxed{S\,n(d_1)=Ke^{-r\tau}n(d_2)}
\]

follows from the definitions of \(d_1,d_2\). For the call formula,

\[
C_S=N(d_1),
\qquad
C_{SS}=\frac{n(d_1)}{S\sigma\sqrt{\tau}},
\]

and

\[
C_t
=-\frac{S\sigma n(d_1)}{2\sqrt{\tau}}
-rKe^{-r\tau}N(d_2).
\]

Substitution into the PDE gives

\[
\begin{aligned}
&C_t+\frac12\sigma^2S^2C_{SS}+rSC_S-rC\\
={}&
\left[
-\frac{S\sigma n(d_1)}{2\sqrt{\tau}}
-rKe^{-r\tau}N(d_2)
\right]
+\frac{S\sigma n(d_1)}{2\sqrt{\tau}}\\
&+rSN(d_1)
-r\left[
SN(d_1)-Ke^{-r\tau}N(d_2)
\right]\\
=&\,0.
\end{aligned}
\]

The call formula therefore satisfies the PDE in the interior. Put--call
parity and the fact that \(S-Ke^{-r\tau}\) itself satisfies the PDE imply the
same result for the put formula.

### 9.3 Terminal and boundary conditions

As \(\tau\downarrow0\):

- if \(S>K\), then \(d_1,d_2\to+\infty\);
- if \(S<K\), then \(d_1,d_2\to-\infty\); and
- if \(S=K\), then \(d_1,d_2\to0\).

Using the corresponding limits of \(N\),

\[
C(t,S)\to(S-K)^+,
\qquad
P(t,S)\to(K-S)^+.
\]

For fixed \(\tau>0\), as \(S\downarrow0\), both \(d_1,d_2\to-\infty\), so

\[
C(t,S)\to0,
\qquad
P(t,S)\to Ke^{-r\tau}.
\]

As \(S\to\infty\), both \(d_1,d_2\to+\infty\), so

\[
C(t,S)\sim S-Ke^{-r\tau},
\qquad
P(t,S)\to0.
\]

The formulas therefore satisfy the terminal and limiting boundary conditions
stated in Section 3.

### 9.4 Bounds and volatility monotonicity

The expectation representation immediately implies

\[
(S-Ke^{-r\tau})^+\leq C(t,S)\leq S
\]

and

\[
(Ke^{-r\tau}-S)^+\leq P(t,S)\leq Ke^{-r\tau}.
\]

Differentiating either price with respect to \(\sigma\) gives the common
vega

\[
\frac{\partial C}{\partial\sigma}
=\frac{\partial P}{\partial\sigma}
=S n(d_1)\sqrt{\tau}>0.
\]

Thus both prices are strictly increasing in volatility for
\(S,K,\sigma,\tau>0\), a property used later to justify one-dimensional
implied-volatility inversion when an admissible price brackets a root.

## 10. Assumptions and scope

This derivation inherits the W2-01 assumptions: constant \(r\) and
\(\sigma\), no dividends, geometric Brownian stock dynamics, continuous
trading and paths, frictionless self-financing replication, and no
arbitrage. It prices European exercise only. At maturity, the formulas are
understood by their payoff limits because \(d_1\) and \(d_2\) contain
\(\sqrt{\tau}\) in the denominator.

These formulas are the core Track A analytical benchmark. Any validation
against real SPX options must explicitly address dividends, rates, bid--ask
spreads, quote timing, exercise and settlement conventions, and other
contract details. Those empirical complications are not silently absorbed
into the no-dividend formula.

Nothing in this derivation redefines Track B:

- a Black--Scholes implied volatility is obtained by inverting one option's
  pricing equation;
- VIX is quoted as 100 times annualized 30-day expected volatility derived
  from a model-free SPX option strip; and
- the empirical variance-space quantity remains
  \(IVAR_t=(VIX_t/100)^2\).

The exact forward 30-calendar-day primary target, fixed-21-trading-day
robustness, overlap-aware inference, out-of-sample design, sign convention,
and sample endpoints remain unchanged.
